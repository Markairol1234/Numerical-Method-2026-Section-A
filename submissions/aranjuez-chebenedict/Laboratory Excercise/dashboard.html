Laboratory Exercise — Series 1

Numerical exploration of Euler's number e through three classic limits: the compound-interest limit, the derivative of the exponential as a difference quotient, and the exponential (Taylor) series.

Python • Matplotlib • numerically-stable math

Overview

All three exercises approach the same constant, e = 2.718281828459045…, but from three different directions — a limit of a power, a limit of a difference quotient, and an infinite series. Comparing them reveals how fast each route converges and where floating-point precision becomes the limiting factor.

Exercise 1 — Convergence of (1 + 1/n)n to e

Task: Implement (1 + 1/n)n up to the nanosecond and draw a histogram.

limn→∞ ( 1 + 1/n )n = e = 2.718281828…

Concept

If you invest at a 100 % annual rate and compound n times per year, the growth factor after one year is (1 + 1/n)n. Compounding more often always yields more, but the increase shrinks each time, converging to e. Going from yearly (n=1 → 2.0) to daily (n=365 → 2.7146) already captures three decimals; by every second it is stable to seven decimals.

Exercise 2 — Difference quotient (ah − 1)/h → ln(a)

Task: Implement the table for three bases as the step h shrinks, then draw a histogram.

limh→0 ( ah − 1 ) / h = ln(a)

Concept

This is the very definition of the derivative of ax evaluated at x = 0: d/dx [ax] at x=0 = ln(a). The difference quotient is a finite-step estimate of that derivative. As h shrinks, the estimate for each base settles onto its natural logarithm.

Explanation of the result: The three coloured bar-groups (blue a=2, green a=e, red a=3) each converge to their dashed reference line: ln(2)=0.6931, ln(e)=1.0000, ln(3)=1.0986. Notice the biggest correction happens in the very first step (h = 0.1) — the truncation error of a forward difference is O(h), so each 10× reduction in h removes about one decimal digit of error.
Special case a = e: When the base is e itself, the limit equals exactly 1 — the unique base whose exponential passes through slope 1 at the origin. This is why e is the "natural" base of the logarithm.
Numerical caveat: For tiny h, ah is almost 1, so ah − 1 suffers catastrophic cancellation. The script uses expm1(h · ln(a))/h, exploiting the identity ah − 1 = eh ln a − 1, which keeps full accuracy down to h = 1e-7.

Exercise 3 — The exponential series ex = ∑ xn/n!

Task: Implement the series for x = 1, summed up to N = 10,000 terms, then draw a histogram.

ex = ∑n=0∞ xn / n! = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …

Concept

This is the Maclaurin (Taylor) series of the exponential. For x = 1 it becomes the celebrated series for e. The terms 1/n! collapse to zero extremely fast because the factorial grows faster than any exponential, so the partial sums converge super-linearly — far faster than the 1/n rate of Exercise 1.

Explanation of the result: The left histogram shows SN leaping from 2.0 to essentially e within the first ~20 terms. The right log-log plot shows the error diving vertically (super-linear convergence): ~8 digits correct by N = 10, ~13 by N = 15, and full double-precision (≈15–16 digits) by N ≈ 20. Beyond that the error is frozen at the machine-precision floor of 4.44e-16.
Why the error flattens: Once a term 1/n! becomes smaller than machine epsilon (≈2.2e-16), adding it to the running sum changes nothing — the computer cannot represent the difference. So summing to N = 10,000 gives the identical result as N = 20; the remaining ~9,980 terms are numerically invisible.
